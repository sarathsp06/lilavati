The fastest slide is not the straight one

Drop steeply first, then coast: the curve that beats the straight line every time.

Three tracks run from A down to B: a straight line, a circular arc and a cycloid. Press Race and three identical balls are let go at the same moment; the stopwatch tells you who arrives first. Drag A and B about, draw a track of your own, and try to beat the cycloid. You can't, and the steps below show why.

What the brachistochrone is

The brachistochrone is the curve of fastest descent: the track from a point A down to a lower point B along which a ball, pulled only by gravity and sliding without friction, arrives in the least time.

  • The question: Johann Bernoulli set it as a challenge in 1696. The name is Greek for "shortest time".
  • The answer: not the straight line, which is only the shortest in length, but a cycloid: the curve drawn by a point on the rim of a rolling wheel. It drops steeply first, then levels out.
  • Why it wins: a steep start makes the ball fast early, and it keeps that speed for the rest of the trip, so it covers a longer track in less time.
  • Why it matters: it asks for the best whole curve, not one best number. Solving it started the calculus of variations, the maths of finding the best path or shape.

On this page A and B are the two points you drag, and the race asks the question out loud: a straight line, a circular arc and the cycloid, plus any track you draw yourself.

The race

How to play

Drag A and B. Press Draw your own, then drag a line from A to B to add your track (Remove yours takes it away). The race restarts by itself after every finish; Race restarts it now. Gravity changes how fast everything happens, never who wins.

The balls slide without friction. On a smooth track each one's speed depends only on how far it has dropped. A sharp corner knocks speed away: the ball keeps only the part of its speed that points along the new direction, so a square corner stops it dead. No ball can climb higher than A.

Try this

  1. Press Race and watch the stopwatch. The straight track is the shortest of the three, yet the cycloid ball arrives first. Watch the speeds: the cycloid ball is fast long before the others.
  2. Drag B far to the right, only a little below A. The cycloid dips well below B and climbs back up to it. It is now much longer than the straight line, and it still wins.
  3. Draw a track that starts almost level, then drops. Your ball crawls along the flat start with almost no speed, and loses badly. The speed you earn early is used for the whole rest of the trip.
  4. Drop straight down, curve round, then run level to B. That gets full speed as early as possible, so it does well, but its path is long: every part is fast, there is just too much of it. It still loses to the cycloid. Draw it with a sharp square corner instead and the ball stops dead at the corner.
  5. Switch gravity to the Moon. Every time grows by the same factor, √(9.8 ÷ 1.6) ≈ 2.5. The order of arrival never changes: the fastest track is a matter of shape, not of gravity.

Why the cycloid wins, step by step

These pictures follow your A, your B and your tracks above.

1Speed depends only on how far the ball has dropped

With no friction, the ball trades height for speed and nothing is lost. Every metre it drops gives it the same amount of energy, whatever track it is on: g joules for each kilogram, about 9.8 J. That energy shows up as speed: half of the speed squared (½·v²) for each kilogram, where v is the speed.

So ½·v² = g·y, where y is the depth below A, and the speed at depth y is v = √(2·g·y), the square root of two times g times y. Two balls at the same depth on different smooth tracks always move at the same speed. The tracks differ only in when they reach each depth. (A sharp corner breaks this: the ball is knocked against the new direction and keeps only the part of its speed along it, v·cos of the angle it turns through. Draw a jagged track to see it lose.)

2Time is distance divided by speed, piece by piece

Cut the straight track into pieces. On each piece the ball's push is steady, so the piece takes its length divided by the average of the speeds at its two ends. Add the pieces up and you have the whole time.

The first piece is the slow one: it starts from a standstill. That is the whole story in one line. Every piece costs length ÷ speed, and the pieces near the start, where speed is low, cost the most.

3The same sum along any track

The rule does not care what shape the track is. Cut your own drawn track into pieces (or the cycloid, if you have not drawn one), find each piece's length and its average speed from the depths at its ends, and add up length ÷ speed.

Compare the first few pieces with the straight line's. A track that gets deep quickly has fast pieces almost from the start; a track that stays shallow pays a slow piece after slow piece.

4Dropping steeply first buys speed for the whole trip

Here is each ball's speed against time, read off the race above. The cycloid starts straight down, so its speed shoots up at once; the straight line gains speed slowly and evenly.

The area under each curve is the distance covered. The cycloid has the longest track, but its curve is high from the very start, so it fills its longer distance sooner. The straight line is short but spends too long being slow.

5The fastest path bends like light

Johann Bernoulli's trick: cut the space below A into thin level layers. In each layer the ball has roughly one speed, faster the deeper you go. Light crossing layers of different speed takes the fastest route, and it bends by Snell's law: call the angle between the path and straight down α (alpha); then sin(α) ÷ speed is the same number in every layer.

Make the ball do the same. Its path is a chain of straight pieces, each a little more tilted than the last. Thin the layers and the chain smooths into one curve: the cycloid. Where the path turns level it has reached the cycloid's lowest point, and if B is beyond it, it climbs back up by the same rule in reverse.

6The cycloid is the mark of a point on a rolling wheel

Stick a pen to the rim of a wheel and roll the wheel along a line. The pen draws a cycloid: it starts straight down from the line, swings along, and comes back up to touch it again. The wheel's radius, called a, sets the size; the angle it has turned, θ (theta), says where the pen is.

For your A and B there is exactly one wheel size that makes the pen pass through B. Roll it from A at a steady √(g ÷ a) radians per second and the pen keeps pace with the racing ball the whole way. So the time to B is the final angle divided by that rate: θ·√(a ÷ g), θ times the square root of a over g, where θ is how far the wheel has turned when the pen reaches B.

In symbols

½·v² = g·y so v = √(2·g·y)

In words: v is the ball's speed, y is how far below A it is, and g is the strength of gravity (9.8 m/s² on Earth). Half of v squared equals g times y, so the speed is the square root of two times g times y: it depends only on depth, not on the track.

T = Σ (length of a piece) ÷ (average speed on that piece) → T = ∫ ds ÷ √(2·g·y)

In words: T is the total time. Σ means "add up over all the pieces"; ∫ means the same with ever thinner pieces, where ds is the tiny length of one piece and √(2·g·y) is the speed there. The brachistochrone is the track that makes T smallest.

sin(α) ÷ v = the same number in every layer

In words: Snell's law. α is the angle between the track and straight down, v is the speed there. Along the fastest track, sine of α divided by v never changes, as for a light ray bending through layers of faster and faster glass.

x = a·(θ − sin θ), y = a·(1 − cos θ), T = θ·√(a ÷ g)

In words: the cycloid. a is the radius of the rolling wheel, θ (theta) is the angle it has turned, in radians, and x, y is where the rim point is: a times (θ minus sine of θ) across, a times (1 minus cosine of θ) down. The wheel size a and the final θ are fixed by where B is; the time to get there is θ times the square root of a over g.

Where you meet it

  • Roller coasters and water slides. The first drop is the steepest part of the ride for a reason: the speed earned there carries you through the rest.
  • Skiing and skateboarding. A racer picking a line down a slope, or a skater dropping into a half-pipe, is choosing a path for speed, not shortness.
  • The lifeguard's run. Running is faster than swimming, so a lifeguard who heads for a drowning swimmer should run further along the beach and swim less. That is Snell's law, and the same maths as step 5.
  • Lenses and mirages. Light takes the fastest path through glass and warm air. Lenses are shaped so every path from an object to its image takes the same time.
  • Rockets and robots. Finding the best whole path (least fuel, least time) rather than the best next step is the calculus of variations, and it began with this problem. Spacecraft trajectories and robot arm motions are planned this way.
  • Pendulum clocks. The same cycloid makes a pendulum swing in the same time whatever its width, which is why Huygens built it into the first accurate clocks.

Fun facts

A challenge to the world, 1696

Johann Bernoulli posed the problem in the journal Acta Eruditorum in June 1696, addressed to "the most brilliant mathematicians in the world", and gave them six months. Leibniz persuaded him to extend the deadline so that foreigners had a chance. The name is Greek: brachistos, shortest, and chronos, time.

Newton did it in one night

Newton learned of the challenge at 4 pm on 29 January 1697, coming home tired from his work at the Royal Mint, and had solved it by 4 the next morning. His answer was published without his name. Bernoulli wrote that he knew the author anyway, "ex ungue leonem": the lion by his claw.

Five answers, one curve

Solutions came from Newton, Leibniz, Jakob Bernoulli (Johann's brother and rival), Tschirnhaus and l'Hôpital. Johann printed his own in Acta Eruditorum in May 1697, in the same issue as most of theirs. Every one of them found the cycloid.

Galileo guessed wrong

In his Two New Sciences of 1638, Galileo showed that sliding along an arc of a circle is quicker than along its chord, and concluded that the circle was the quickest path of all. He was right that the straight line loses, and wrong about the winner: the circle is beaten by the cycloid.

The same curve keeps time

Christiaan Huygens found in 1659 that a ball let go anywhere on a cycloid reaches the bottom in the same time (the tautochrone). In his 1673 book Horologium Oscillatorium he fitted pendulum clocks with cycloid-shaped cheeks so that wide and narrow swings took the same time.

Bernoulli's solution was borrowed light

Johann Bernoulli solved the problem by pretending the ball was a ray of light passing through layers of ever thinner air, so that Snell's law of refraction did the work. That is step 5 above, and it is still the quickest route to the answer.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "The shortest path is the fastest." But: the straight line is shortest and loses every race. Time is length divided by speed, and the straight line is slow for too long.
  • Not: "Then the steepest start must win: fall straight down and run level." But: that gets speed early, but the path is long: a full drop plus the whole way across. Try it above: the cycloid still wins.
  • Not: "A heavier ball wins." But: mass cancels out, as it does for falling objects. Identical or not, balls on the same track arrive together.
  • Not: "The cycloid wins because it ends up going faster." But: at B every ball has the same speed, √(2·g·depth). The cycloid wins because it got its speed earlier.
