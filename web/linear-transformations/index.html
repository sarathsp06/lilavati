It stretches, turns and folds space, and a matrix is the recipe.

Drag the green arrow î and the red arrow ĵ, and the whole grid follows. The four numbers of the matrix are just where those two arrows end up. Every layer of a neural network does this to its data, in many more dimensions, millions of times a second.

How a matrix moves space, step by step

These pictures follow what you set up above: change it and they change.

1Follow the two arrows and you know where everything goes

Any vector v = (x, y) is x steps of î plus y steps of ĵ. A linear move keeps that recipe: after the move, v lands at x steps of the new î plus y steps of the new ĵ.

That is why two arrows are enough to describe the whole move, and why a 2×2 matrix only needs four numbers.

2Lines stay lines, and the middle stays put

"Linear" means: grid lines stay straight, stay parallel, stay evenly spaced, and the origin doesn't move. Squares become parallelograms, all the same.

Moves that bend lines, or slide everything sideways, are not linear. A shift added afterwards is called affine; neural networks add one, the bias.

3The matrix is the two landing spots, written as columns

Write where î lands as the first column and where ĵ lands as the second. Multiplying the matrix by a vector (x, y) means: x times the first column plus y times the second. Row by row, that is a dot product of each row with the vector.

Some matrices have names. A diagonal one only stretches along the axes. A symmetric one has the same number above and below the diagonal. An orthogonal one only turns or mirrors, keeping every length.

4One move after another is matrix multiplication

Doing B first and then A is a single move, the matrix product AB. Read right to left: the move nearest the vector happens first.

Order matters. Turning and then stretching is not the same as stretching and then turning, so AB and BA are usually different matrices.

5Undoing a move is the inverse matrix

If a move can be undone, the matrix that undoes it is the inverse, A⁻¹. Do A and then A⁻¹, and every point is back where it started.

Solving equations is the same question backwards: which v lands on b, so that Av = b? If A can be undone, v = A⁻¹b. If A squashes space onto a line, there is no inverse, and most b can't be reached at all.

6A layer of a neural network: a matrix, then a bend

A neural network layer computes W x + b: a matrix move and a shift. On its own that could only ever stretch and turn the data. So after it comes a bend, usually ReLU: any negative number becomes 0.

The bend folds space along the axes. Stack layers, move-fold-move-fold, and a network can untangle data no single matrix could.

In symbols

A v = x (first column) + y (second column)

In words: a matrix times a vector mixes the columns in the amounts the vector says.

[[a, b], [c, d]] (x, y) = (ax + by, cx + dy)

In words: the same, row by row: each row dotted with the vector.

(AB) v = A (B v)

In words: multiplying matrices means doing one move after the other, right to left.

AB ≠ BA

In words: usually; the order of the moves matters.

A⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · [[d, −b], [−c, a]]

In words: the inverse of a 2×2 matrix; it doesn't exist when ad − bc = 0.

y = ReLU(W x + b)

In words: one neural network layer: move, shift, then fold negatives to zero.

Where you meet it

  • Neural networks. Every layer multiplies its input vector by a weight matrix. Training a model means finding good numbers for those matrices.
  • Graphics cards. Every frame of a 3D game turns and projects millions of corners of shapes with 4×4 matrices. Chips built for that turned out to be perfect for AI.
  • Photos and video. Turning, flipping and skewing an image are matrices. Converting colours from RGB to the brightness-and-colour form video uses is a 3×3 matrix.
  • Robots. Each joint of a robot arm is a turn; chaining the matrices tells where the hand is.
  • Solving equations. Engineering software solves systems like Ax = b with thousands of unknowns, for bridges, circuits and weather models.

Fun facts

The name "matrix", 1850

James Joseph Sylvester coined the word "matrix" in 1850. Arthur Cayley wrote the first paper treating matrices as things you can add and multiply, in 1858.

8 multiplications or 7?

Multiplying two 2×2 matrices the school way takes 8 multiplications. In 1969 Volker Strassen found a way with 7, which speeds up multiplying huge matrices.

An AI found faster ways

In 2022 DeepMind's AlphaTensor searched for new matrix multiplication methods and found one for 4×4 matrices (in arithmetic where 1 + 1 = 0) using 47 multiplications instead of the 49 Strassen's method needs.

175 billion numbers

GPT-3 (2020) has 175 billion parameters, and almost all of them are entries of weight matrices.

Graphics chips became AI chips

Graphics cards were built to multiply matrices for games. Around 2012, researchers found they also train neural networks many times faster than ordinary processors.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "AB is the same as BA." But: the order of moves matters, so they are usually different.
  • Not: "A matrix is just a table of numbers." But: it is also a move of space. The columns tell you where the basic arrows go.
  • Not: "y = 2x + 3 is linear, so it's a linear transformation." But: it shifts the origin, so in this sense it is affine: a linear move plus a shift.
