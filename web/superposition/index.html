How noise-cancelling headphones make silence

Add a sound to its mirror image and you hear nothing at all.

Mix up to three waves and watch, and listen to, what their sum does. Two equal waves can double each other or cancel to silence. Two notes slightly out of tune throb in loudness. A few waves at whole-number frequencies make a chord, or even the corners of a square wave.

What superposition is

Superposition is the rule that waves meeting in the same place simply add up.

  • Heights add. At every moment and every place, the height of the combined wave is the height of one wave plus the height of the other. A dip counts as a negative height.
  • The waves pass through each other unchanged. After they meet, each carries on as if the other had never been there.
  • So the sum can be bigger or smaller. A crest on a crest makes a taller crest; a crest on an equal dip makes nothing at all.

It holds for sound, light, water ripples and radio, as long as the waves are not extremely strong. The picture just below adds two slow waves moment by moment. After it, the waves are sounds that go up and down hundreds of times a second, and you can hear the sum: a sound plus its mirror image is silence, which is how noise-cancelling headphones work.

The idea on two waves

Two waves, A and B, both 1 high. Drag the moment along: at each moment, take A's height and B's height and stack one on the other. That point is the sum. Do it at every moment and the black wave appears.

Read the first line as: t is how far along you are, in laps of A; 360°·t means 360 degrees times t, so A goes up and down once per lap, and B goes up and down 1, 2 or 3 times per lap (the number after 360°), started the shift further round. Below, the same adding happens with sound waves, hundreds of laps a second.

The waves

Frequency: how many times a second it goes up and down (Hz). Size: how tall. Shift: how far along its cycle it starts.

Size 0 switches a wave off. Listening plays the real sound, quietly.

Try this

  1. Pick "Cancel out". Two identical waves, one shifted by half a cycle. Every peak meets a dip, so the sum is a flat line: silence. Press Listen.
  2. Pick "Beats" and listen. 220 Hz and 224 Hz together throb 4 times a second: the difference between them. Musicians tune by listening for this.
  3. Slide wave B from 224 Hz up to 230 Hz. The throbbing speeds up to 10 a second. Bring it back to 220 and the throbbing stops: they're in tune.
  4. Pick "Towards a square wave". Three smooth waves at 1, 3 and 5 times the frequency, sized 1, ⅓ and ⅕, already show flat tops and steep sides. That's a Fourier series built by ear.

How waves add up, step by step

These pictures follow the waves you set up above.

1Add them up, moment by moment

Pick any moment. Wave A is at some height (the blue arrow), wave B at another (orange). The sum at that moment is one height stacked on top of the other. If B is below the line, it pulls the total down.

Do that at every moment and the black line appears. There's no other rule: superposition just means "add the heights".

2Same frequency: the shift decides everything

A wave is the height of an arrow spinning round a circle. Two waves at the same frequency are two arrows spinning together, so put the second on the tip of the first: the black arrow from the centre to the tip is the sum, and it spins too.

Its length comes from the triangle the two arrows make. In step (shift 0°) they point the same way and the sum is a + b. Half a cycle apart (180°) they point opposite ways and the sum is a − b: equal waves cancel completely.

3Nearly the same frequency: beats

If B spins slightly faster than A, it slowly laps it. Sometimes the two arrows line up and the sum is big; half a lap later they point opposite ways and the sum nearly vanishes.

That makes the loudness swell and fade, as many times a second as B gains a full lap on A: the difference between the two frequencies. 224 − 220 = 4 beats per second.

4Whole-number frequencies build shapes

Waves at 1, 2, 3 … times a base frequency repeat together, so their sum repeats too: it has a shape. Musical instruments sound different because they mix these harmonics in different amounts.

Take only the odd ones, 1, 3, 5, 7 …, with sizes 1, ⅓, ⅕, 1/7 … Each new wave flattens the tops a bit more and steepens the sides, closing in on a square wave. That is exactly what a Fourier series does.

5Two waves going opposite ways: a standing wave

Send one wave right and the same wave left, and add them. Some points always get a peak from one wave and a dip from the other at the same moment: they never move. These are nodes. Halfway between, the two waves always agree and the string swings widest.

A guitar string pinned at both ends can only hold the waves whose nodes land on its ends: that's why it plays one note and its harmonics.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: y(t) is the height of the wave at time t, a its size, f its frequency in cycles per second, φ (phi) its starting phase. 2π·f·t means 2π times f times t. sin(3·x) is the sine of three times x, a wave three times as fast. k and ω (omega) are how fast a wave repeats in space and in time.

y(t) = a · sin(2π·f·t + φ)

In words: one wave of size a, f cycles a second, started φ of the way round its cycle.

y_total(t) = y₁(t) + y₂(t) + y₃(t)

In words: superposition: at every moment, the heights add.

A = √(a² + b² + 2·a·b·cos φ)

In words: two waves of the same frequency add to one wave; its size is the third side of the arrow triangle (the law of cosines).

f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|

In words: two nearly equal notes throb as many times a second as their frequencies differ.

sin x + ⅓ sin(3·x) + ⅕ sin(5·x) + … = π/4 · square(x)

In words: odd harmonics, smaller and smaller, add up to a square wave.

sin(k·x − ω·t) + sin(k·x + ω·t) = 2 · sin(k·x) · cos(ω·t)

In words: two waves going opposite ways make a standing wave: a fixed shape, sin(k·x), whose size swings with time.

Where you meet it

  • Noise-cancelling headphones. Microphones hear the noise outside and the speaker plays the same sound shifted by half a cycle, so the two cancel at your ear.
  • Tuning instruments. Play two strings together and listen for beats; when the throbbing slows down to nothing, they are in tune.
  • Music and voices. Every note from a piano, violin or voice is a base wave plus harmonics; their mix is what makes a flute sound different from a trumpet.
  • Soap bubbles and oil on water. Light reflected from the top and the bottom of the thin film adds up; some colours cancel and others are boosted, giving the rainbow swirls.
  • Wi-Fi and 5G antennas. Several antennas send the same signal with small shifts so the waves add up towards your phone and cancel elsewhere (beamforming).

Fun facts

Light adding up

Around 1801–1803 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw bright and dark stripes: light waves adding and cancelling. It settled that light behaves as a wave.

Tuning to A

The standard tuning note A above middle C is 440 Hz, set as international standard ISO 16. An orchestra tunes to it, often listening for beats.

Helmholtz and harmony

In 1863 Hermann von Helmholtz published On the Sensations of Tone, explaining why some note combinations sound rough: their harmonics beat against each other.

Hearing a black-hole merger

LIGO detected gravitational waves in 2015 by splitting a laser beam, sending it down two 4 km arms and adding it back together. A ripple in space shifted the beams by a tiny fraction of a cycle.

Pythagoras and string lengths

The Pythagoreans noticed that strings whose lengths are in simple ratios, 2:1 (an octave) or 3:2 (a fifth), sound pleasant together. Their harmonics line up instead of beating.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "Two sounds together are always louder." But: half a cycle apart, two equal sounds cancel to silence. Where the energy goes: it is louder somewhere else in the room.
  • Not: "Beats are a third note." But: they are the loudness of the sum swelling and fading. You hear one note that throbs.
  • Not: "Waves always just add." But: only while they are small enough for the material to respond in proportion. Very loud sounds, or ocean waves breaking on a beach, stop adding simply.
