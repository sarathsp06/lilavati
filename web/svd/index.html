How to shrink a photo to a few numbers

Any matrix is a turn, a stretch and another turn. Keep the big stretches.

Any matrix at all, however messy, sends a circle to an ellipse. The SVD finds the three simple moves that do it: turn, stretch along the axes, turn again. Slide through the three moves below, then scroll to see the same idea compress a picture and find the main direction in a cloud of data (PCA), and why modern AI fine-tuning keeps only the biggest stretches.

What the singular value decomposition is

The singular value decomposition (SVD) splits any matrix into three simple moves done one after another: a turn, a stretch along the axes, and another turn.

  • The first turn, Vᵀ ("V transposed"), lines up the two directions the matrix stretches most and least with the axes.
  • The stretch, Σ (capital sigma), scales each axis by its own number: σ₁ along the first, σ₂ along the second. These are the singular values: never negative, biggest first.
  • The second turn, U, swings the stretched result into its final place. (Either turn may include a mirror flip.)
  • Written together: A = U·Σ·Vᵀ, read right to left: first Vᵀ, then Σ, then U. Keeping only the biggest σ gives the best simpler version of the matrix, which is how a photo shrinks to a few numbers.

Taking any matrix apart, step by step

These pictures follow what you set up above: change it and they change.

1A circle always becomes an ellipse

Whatever the matrix, the unit circle lands on an ellipse (or, if the matrix squashes, a line segment). The ellipse has a longest radius σ₁ and a shortest σ₂: the singular values.

2Two arrows at right angles stay at right angles

On the circle there are two arrows at right angles, v₁ and v₂, that land exactly on the ellipse's axes: A v₁ = σ₁·u₁ and A v₂ = σ₂·u₂ (σ₁ times the arrow u₁), where u₁ and u₂ are also at right angles.

Eigenvectors only exist for some matrices and need not be at right angles. These singular vectors always exist, for any matrix, even a non-square one.

3Three simple moves: turn, stretch, turn

So A = U Σ Vᵀ. First Vᵀ turns v₁ and v₂ onto the axes. Then Σ stretches along the axes by σ₁ and σ₂. Then U turns the axes onto u₁ and u₂. (If the matrix mirrors, one stretch also flips.)

4Keep the big stretches, drop the small ones

A picture is a grid of numbers, a matrix. Its SVD writes it as a sum of simple layers, each one column times one row, ordered by size σ. Keeping only the first few layers gives the best possible approximation with that few numbers (the Eckart–Young theorem).

A 48×48 picture has 2,304 numbers; 5 layers need only 485. Modern AI uses the same idea: LoRA fine-tunes giant language models by learning only small low-rank changes to their weight matrices.

5PCA: the main directions of data

Take a cloud of data points. The SVD of the centred data, or equally the eigenvectors of its covariance matrix, gives the directions it varies most: the principal components. Projecting onto the first one keeps the most information in a single number.

Here the cloud is a round blob moved by your matrix, so PCA should find your matrix's u₁ and u₂, and it does.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: A is any matrix. U and V are turns, Σ (capital sigma, here a matrix, not a sum) is the stretch, and Vᵀ is V run backwards. σ₁, σ₂, … ("sigma sub 1") are the stretch factors, biggest first. uᵢ and vᵢ are the i-th arrows of U and V, and σ₁·u₁·v₁ᵀ is one layer: a number times an outer product of two arrows.

A = U Σ Vᵀ

In words: turn, stretch, turn: works for every matrix, any shape.

σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0

In words: the stretches, biggest first; never negative.

A vᵢ = σᵢ uᵢ

In words: each right singular vector lands on a left one, stretched by σᵢ.

Aₖ = σ₁·u₁·v₁ᵀ + … + σₖ·uₖ·vₖᵀ

In words: the best approximation using k layers; the error is the dropped σ²s.

σᵢ² = eigenvalues of AᵀA

In words: how to compute them: the SVD is eigenvectors of AᵀA and AAᵀ.

A⁺ = V Σ⁺ Uᵀ

In words: the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse: flip each non-zero σ to 1/σ. It gives the best possible "undo", even for squashing matrices.

Where you meet it

  • Compressing data. Keeping only the biggest singular values shrinks pictures, sensor data and text tables with little loss.
  • Recommendations. A table of users × films is approximated by a low-rank product: a few hidden "tastes" per user and per film.
  • Fine-tuning AI models. LoRA adapts a huge language model by learning low-rank updates to its weight matrices, a tiny fraction of the parameters.
  • Search. Latent semantic analysis (1990) used the SVD of a word-by-document table to find documents about the same topic with different words.
  • Fitting data. Least-squares fitting, finding the best line through noisy points, is solved reliably with the pseudoinverse built from the SVD.

Fun facts

Found twice

Eugenio Beltrami (1873) and Camille Jordan (1874) discovered the SVD independently of each other.

Best low-rank picture, 1936

Carl Eckart and Gale Young proved in 1936 that cutting the SVD after k terms gives the best possible rank-k approximation.

PCA, 1901

Karl Pearson described principal component analysis in 1901, as finding the line that best fits a cloud of points.

A million-dollar prize

Netflix offered $1,000,000 for a 10% better film recommender. Low-rank matrix factorisation, close to the SVD, was at the heart of the winning BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos team in 2009.

10,000 times fewer

The 2021 LoRA paper reported fine-tuning GPT-3 while training about 10,000 times fewer parameters, by learning low-rank matrix updates.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "The SVD only works for square matrices." But: it works for every matrix, of any shape.
  • Not: "Singular values can be negative." But: they never are; a mirror is put in one of the turns (or, as here, shown as a flipped stretch).
  • Not: "Singular values are the same as eigenvalues." But: only for symmetric matrices with no negative eigenvalues. A turn has singular values 1 and 1 but no real eigenvalues.
