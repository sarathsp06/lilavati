The Monty Hall problem

Why you should always switch doors

Three doors, one car, one goat revealed. Switching wins twice as often, and you can prove it by playing.

Pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door with a goat behind it and offers you the one door left. Play by hand, let thousands of games play themselves, try 100 doors, or make the host guess, and watch how often staying and switching win.

What the Monty Hall problem is

The Monty Hall problem is a puzzle about chance: after a host who knows the answer opens a losing door, should you keep your door or switch?

  • The game: three closed doors, a car behind one and a goat behind each of the other two. You pick a door.
  • The host's rule: the host (Monty Hall, of the American TV show Let's Make a Deal) knows where the car is. He always opens one of the two doors you didn't pick, always one with a goat, and offers you the last closed door.
  • The answer: switching wins 2 games in 3, staying only 1 in 3. Two closed doors look like 50/50, but they aren't.
  • The lesson: what a clue tells you depends on how it was chosen. The host is not opening a door at random; he must avoid the car, and that is information. Updating chances like this is Bayes' rule.

Below you play the game itself: pick a door, watch the host, stay or switch, then let thousands of games run and watch the 1 in 3 and 2 in 3 appear. The steps then work both numbers out by drawing every possible game.

Try this

  1. Let it play itself for a minute. The two lines wobble at first, then settle: staying near 1/3, switching near 2/3. Neither is 1/2.
  2. Play ten games by hand and always stay. You win about three of them. Then play ten and always switch: about seven. Small runs are noisy; the lines above are the long run.
  3. Pick 100 doors. You pick one, the host opens 98 goats and leaves exactly one other door shut. Would you really stay? Switching wins 99 times in 100.
  4. Make the host open doors at random, then play 1000 games. About a third of the games he shows the car and they don't count. In the rest, staying and switching win equally often: 1/2 each. The host's knowledge is what made switching better.

From your first pick to two in three, step by step

These pictures use the number of doors and the game above: your pick and the door the host left shut.

1Your first pick is right one time in n

Before anything happens, the car is equally likely to be behind any door. Picture each of those possibilities as a separate world: with 3 doors there are 3 worlds, each with chance 1/3.

Walk the car through every world and count the ones where it is behind your door: just one. So your first pick is right with chance 1/n, and wrong with chance (n − 1)/n. Nothing the host does later can change which world you are in.

2The host's hands are tied, except in one world

The host must open every other door except one, and never the car. In a world where the car is behind another door, he has no choice at all: he must leave that door shut.

Only in the world where your first pick is right can he choose which goat door to leave shut, and he picks among them at random. Watch each world: one row keeps changing its mind, the others always end the same way.

3Cross out the worlds that don't match what you saw

Draw every world as a piece of a square with area equal to its chance. Your door's column (1/n wide) is cut into n − 1 slices, one for each door the host might leave shut. Every other column is one block: there the host's move is forced.

You saw him leave one particular door shut. Cross out every piece where he would have done something else. Two pieces survive: a thin slice where you were right (stay wins) and a whole column where the car is behind the door he kept (switch wins). Blow them back up to fill the square: 1 part to n − 1, so 1/3 against 2/3 with three doors.

4Switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong

With a host who knows, the door he leaves shut hides the car whenever your first pick missed it. So "switch" is the same bet as "my first pick was wrong", and "stay" is the same bet as "my first pick was right".

Deal a stack of games and sort them by one question only: was the first pick right? The piles grow at 1 to n − 1, the same split as step 1.

5A host who guesses gives you nothing: 1/2

Now let the host open doors at random. In every world, including the ones where your pick is wrong, he could leave any of the other doors shut, so every column is cut into slices.

Seeing goats behind the doors he opened rules out more: in the worlds where the car was behind a door he opened, he would have shown it (red). The two surviving slices are the same size, so stay and switch are 1/2 each. Same doors, same goats on show, different answer: what matters is how the door was chosen.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: n is the number of doors. P(A) is the chance of A, a number from 0 to 1. P(A | B) is read "the chance of A given B": the chance of A once you know B happened. C₂ means "the car is behind door 2"; H₃ means "the host opens door 3". You picked door 1.

P(first pick right) = 1/n

In words: one world in n has the car behind your door. With three doors, 1/3.

P(switch wins) = (n − 1)/n

In words: a knowing host leaves the car's door shut whenever you missed it, so switching wins whenever your first pick was wrong. Three doors: 2/3; a hundred: 99/100.

P(C₂ | H₃) = P(H₃ | C₂) · P(C₂) / P(H₃) = (1 · ⅓) / ½ = ⅔

In words: Bayes' rule. If the car is behind door 2 the host must open door 3 (chance 1); the car is there a third of the time; he opens door 3 half of the time overall. That gives two thirds.

random host: P(switch wins | only goats shown) = ½

In words: if the host might have shown the car but didn't, the two surviving worlds are equally likely, whatever n is.

Where you meet it

  • Medical tests. A positive result means different things depending on how common the illness is and why you were tested: the same updating of chances on evidence as the host's door.
  • Card games. Bridge players use the "principle of restricted choice": an opponent who played a card because they had to tells you more than one who chose it freely. It is the Monty Hall argument at the card table.
  • Search and rescue. Searchers split the sea into squares with chances and update them after each square is searched and found empty. Bayesian search helped find the wreck of Air France flight 447 in 2011.
  • Spam filters. Each word in an email shifts the chance that it is spam, using Bayes' rule, the same rule as step 3.
  • Reading reviews and surveys. Who chose to answer, or which results were chosen to show you, changes what they tell you, just as a host who knows differs from one who guesses.

Fun facts

10,000 letters

In September 1990 Marilyn vos Savant answered the puzzle in her Ask Marilyn column in Parade magazine: switch. About 10,000 readers wrote in, nearly 1,000 of them with PhDs, most saying she was wrong. She was right.

Named in 1975

The statistician Steve Selvin posed the problem in letters to The American Statistician in 1975 and called it the "Monty Hall problem". Monty Hall wrote to him about it the same year.

Erdős wasn't convinced

Paul Erdős, one of the most prolific mathematicians ever, refused to believe the answer until he was shown a computer simulation, as Andrew Vázsonyi recounted. Which is what the Play section above does.

Pigeons do better

In a 2010 study, Walter Herbranson and Julia Schroeder found that pigeons playing a version of the game with grain as the prize quickly learned to switch nearly every time. People in the same kind of test did not.

Older cousins

Joseph Bertrand's box paradox (1889) and Martin Gardner's three prisoners problem (Scientific American, 1959) are the same trap in other clothes; both give the "obvious" 1/2 where the right answer is 2/3 or 1/3.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "Two doors are left, so it's 50/50." But: the two doors didn't get there the same way. Yours was picked blind; the other survived a host who was steering around the car.
  • Not: "Opening a door moves the car's chances around." But: the car never moves. What changes is what you know about which world you are in.
  • Not: "Switching always wins." But: it wins 2 times in 3. A third of the time your first pick was right and switching loses the car.
  • Not: "It doesn't matter whether the host knows." But: a host who opens a door at random and happens to show a goat leaves you at 1/2 (step 5).
