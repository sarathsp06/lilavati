Try this

  1. Press "Faster" three times on the space station. The orbit stretches into an ellipse, but the high point appears on the opposite side of the Earth, half a lap later. Where you burn stays the low point.
  2. Launch at 142% speed. That's escape velocity. The path never closes: it slows down as it climbs, but never quite stops, and never comes back.
  3. Launch at 80% speed. Not enough sideways speed to miss the Earth. It falls in an ellipse that cuts into the ground.
  4. Build a 35,786 km tower and let go of a ball. It doesn't fall. The top of the tower already moves at exactly orbital speed: that's a geostationary orbit.
  5. Now a 2,000 km tower. Gravity at the top is only about 58% of normal, yet the ball still falls straight back. Height alone doesn't get you into orbit; sideways speed does.

Why things orbit, step by step

These pictures follow the satellite and tower in the toy above.

1Throw it hard enough and it misses the ground

Newton imagined a cannon on a very tall mountain. Fire the ball slowly and it lands nearby. Fire it faster and it lands further away, and because the Earth is round, the ground curves away beneath it as it falls.

Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away exactly as fast as the ball falls. It never lands: it's in orbit. Faster still and the path stretches into a long ellipse, and finally it doesn't come back at all.

2Gravity gets weaker with distance, but slowly

Gravity pulls with a strength that falls with the square of the distance from the Earth's centre. Double the distance and the pull is a quarter.

The centre is about 6,400 km below you, so going up 400 km to the space station barely changes anything: gravity there is still about 89% of what you feel now. Astronauts float because they are falling, not because gravity is gone.

3The speed for a circle: gravity does the turning

Anything moving in a circle needs a pull towards the middle of v²/r. For a satellite, gravity is that pull. Set them equal and you get the one speed that makes a circle at each height.

Higher up, gravity is weaker, so a circle needs less speed. The space station goes 7.7 km/s; the Moon only about 1 km/s. If your satellite is below the green line it dips inwards; above it, it swings out; above the red line, it escapes.

4Change the speed and you change the far side

Speed up and you're moving a bit too fast for a circle, so you swing outwards, climb, slow down as you climb, and fall back round to where you started. The point where you fired becomes your lowest point, and the highest point is exactly opposite.

Slow down and the opposite side dips. Slow down enough and the dip goes into the ground. That's how spacecraft come home: a short braking burn, half an orbit before the place they want to land.

5Escape velocity: enough energy to climb out

Think of gravity as a deep well. Climbing out takes energy, and the satellite carries its energy as speed: ½v² for each kilogram. The well's depth at distance r is GM/r for each kilogram.

If the speed energy is less than the depth, the total is negative and the satellite is trapped: it orbits. If it's exactly equal, the total is zero and it just makes it out: that speed is escape velocity, √2 ≈ 1.41 times circle speed. The direction doesn't matter, as long as it doesn't hit the Earth on the way.

6A tower into space

Stand on a tower at the equator and the Earth's spin carries you round once a day. The higher the tower, the faster its top moves, while gravity gets weaker. Some of gravity is used up just turning you round in a circle, so a scale reads less than gravity alone.

At 35,786 km the two match exactly. You'd weigh nothing, and a ball let go there stays put: it's in geostationary orbit. Above that, the spin wins: you'd have to hang on to the tower or be flung outwards. This is the idea behind a space elevator.

In symbols

F = G M m / r²

In words: the pull between the Earth (mass M) and a satellite (mass m) falls with the square of the distance r between their centres.

v_circle = √(GM / r)

In words: the speed for a circular orbit. The satellite's own mass cancels out, so a pebble and a space station at the same height go equally fast.

v_escape = √(2GM / r) = √2 · v_circle

In words: escape speed is about 41% more than circle speed at the same height. From the ground it is 11.2 km/s.

E = ½v² − GM / r

In words: energy per kilogram. Negative means trapped in an orbit; zero or more means it escapes.

T = 2π √(a³ / GM)

In words: Kepler's third law. The time for one lap depends only on the size of the orbit (a, half its longest width): higher orbits take longer.

v² = GM (2/r − 1/a)

In words: the vis-viva equation. Your speed at any point of an orbit, from how far out you are and how big the orbit is.

Where you meet it

  • Satellite TV and weather pictures. Geostationary satellites, 35,786 km up, go round once a day, so they hang over the same spot and a dish can point at them without moving.
  • The map on your phone. GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 km up, twice a day. Your phone times their signals to work out where you are.
  • The bright dot crossing the night sky. That's often the space station, about 400 km up, lapping the Earth every 92 minutes.
  • Why rockets launch eastwards. The Earth's spin already gives them up to 465 m/s at the equator for free, so they launch the way it turns.
  • Space probes. Every mission to the Moon, Mars or beyond first has to reach escape velocity, or use a planet's gravity to swing past it.

Fun facts

16 sunrises a day

The space station goes round the Earth about every 92 minutes at around 7.7 km/s, so the crew sees about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day.

Newton's cannon

The cannon on a mountain comes from Isaac Newton's "A Treatise of the System of the World", published in 1728, the year after his death. It is the same idea as step 1.

A sci-fi writer's idea

Arthur C. Clarke described using satellites in geostationary orbit to relay radio and TV around the world in a 1945 magazine article, "Extra-Terrestrial Relays". It's sometimes called the Clarke orbit.

A tower to the sky, in 1895

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, inspired by the Eiffel Tower, imagined a tower reaching up to geostationary height in 1895. Today's space elevator ideas use a cable instead, because no material is strong enough for a tower.

Lighter at the top of the Burj Khalifa

At the top of the 828 m Burj Khalifa, gravity is about 0.026% weaker than at its foot. A 70 kg person would weigh about 18 grams less. The building's own pull on you is far smaller still.

GPS has to correct for time

The clocks on GPS satellites run about 38 microseconds a day fast compared with clocks on the ground, because of Einstein's relativity. Without correcting for it, GPS would drift by kilometres a day.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "There's no gravity in space." But: at the space station gravity is still about 89% of what it is on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station are falling together.
  • Not: "To catch up with a satellite ahead of you, speed up." But: speeding up lifts you into a bigger, slower orbit and you fall behind. You slow down, drop lower, and go round faster.
  • Not: "Escape velocity means going straight up." But: it's about energy, not direction. Any direction works, as long as the path doesn't hit the Earth first.
  • Not: "Heavier satellites have to go faster." But: the mass cancels out. A feather and a space station at the same height orbit at the same speed.
