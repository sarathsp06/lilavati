The determinant

The determinant says how much a matrix stretches area, and whether it flips space over.

The shaded square starts with area 1. Drag î and ĵ and watch it become a parallelogram: its area is the determinant. Every other shape grows or shrinks by the same factor. If the colour turns red, space has been flipped over like a mirror image; if the area hits zero, space has been squashed flat.

Try this

  1. Pick "Shear". The square leans over, but its area stays exactly 1. Shears and turns never change area.
  2. Drag ĵ across to the other side of î. Just as it crosses, the area hits 0, then turns red and negative: space has been flipped.
  3. Pick "Make twice as big". Lengths double, but areas grow 4 times: the determinant is 2 × 2 = 4.
  4. Pick "Squash onto a line". Area 0: the whole plane lands on one line, and the circle becomes a line segment.

What the determinant measures, step by step

These pictures follow what you set up above: change it and they change.

1Watch one square

The square between î and ĵ has area 1. After the move it is the parallelogram between the new î and ĵ. Its area is the determinant (ignoring the sign for now).

2Every shape scales by the same amount

A linear move treats every little grid square the same way, and any shape is made of little grid squares. So every area, of a circle, a flag, a country on a map, gets multiplied by the same number.

3Where ad − bc comes from

Put the parallelogram in a box of width a + b and height c + d. Its area is the box minus the corner pieces: two triangles of area ac/2, two of area bd/2 and two rectangles of area bc. What is left is ad − bc.

The same idea in 3D gives the volume of a slanted box, and in any number of dimensions, the "volume" a matrix multiplies by.

4A negative determinant means space is flipped

Normally ĵ is a quarter turn anticlockwise from î. If after the move ĵ is clockwise from î, space has been turned over like a page: every shape becomes its mirror image. The determinant is then negative; its size is still the area factor.

5Zero means squashed flat, and no way back

If the determinant is 0, the two arrows land on the same line and every area becomes 0: the whole plane is squashed onto a line (or a point). Many points land on the same spot, so the move can't be undone: there is no inverse.

In data, this happens when one input is just a mix of others, for example height in cm and height in inches. They add nothing new, and the matrix has a determinant of 0.

6Moves multiply, so determinants multiply

Doing B and then A scales area by det(B) and then by det(A). So det(AB) = det(A) × det(B). Undoing A scales area back, so det(A⁻¹) = 1 / det(A).

In symbols

det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc

In words: the area factor of a 2×2 matrix, with a sign for flipping.

det(AB) = det(A) · det(B)

In words: doing two moves multiplies their area factors.

det(A⁻¹) = 1 / det(A)

In words: undoing a move undoes its area change.

det(A) = 0 ⇔ no inverse

In words: squashed flat means the move can't be undone, and the columns lie on one line.

det(A) = λ₁ · λ₂

In words: the product of the eigenvalues (next chapter).

det(A) = σ₁ · σ₂ (up to sign)

In words: the product of the singular values (last chapter).

Where you meet it

  • Solving equations. A set of equations has exactly one answer only if its matrix has a non-zero determinant.
  • Generative AI. "Normalizing flow" models generate data by stretching simple randomness into complex shapes; the log of the determinant keeps track of how probability gets spread out.
  • Changing coordinates in calculus. Switching to new coordinates (like polar) multiplies every small area by a determinant, the Jacobian.
  • 3D graphics. The sign of a 2D determinant tells whether a triangle's corners go clockwise or anticlockwise on screen, so the back sides of objects can be skipped.
  • Robotics. When a robot arm's determinant reaches zero, it is in a position where it can't move in some direction.

Fun facts

Before matrices existed

Determinants came first. Seki Takakazu used them in Japan in 1683, and Gottfried Leibniz in Europe in 1693, more than 150 years before matrices were named.

Cramer's rule, 1750

Gabriel Cramer published a rule in 1750 that solves equations as ratios of determinants. It is elegant, but far too slow for big systems.

Named by Gauss

Carl Friedrich Gauss used the word "determinant" in 1801, for a different but related number; Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave it its modern meaning in 1812.

Shears keep area

Sliding a stack of cards sideways keeps its volume: Cavalieri's principle (1635). A shear matrix has determinant 1 for the same reason.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "A big determinant means big numbers in the matrix." But: [[100, 100], [100, 100]] has determinant 0: its columns lie on one line.
  • Not: "Turning changes the determinant." But: turning keeps every area, so its determinant is always 1.
  • Not: "Determinant 0 means the matrix is all zeros." But: it only means space is squashed into fewer dimensions.
