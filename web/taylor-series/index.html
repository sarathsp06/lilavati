How your calculator knows sin(37°)

Add up a few simple powers of x and you get the curve back, near one point.

A calculator can only add and multiply, yet it gives you sin(37°) at once. The trick is a polynomial that behaves like the sine curve. Below you can build such polynomials one term at a time for six curves, move the point they are built around, and see how far out they stay good and where some of them stop working altogether.

What a Taylor series is

A Taylor series rebuilds a curve near one point, a, from what the curve is doing at that point: its height, its slope, how fast the slope changes, and so on.

  • The terms: f(a), then f′(a)·(x − a), then f″(a)/2·(x − a)², then f‴(a)/6·(x − a)³, and so on. Each term is a number times a power of (x − a), the distance from a.
  • One more match each: the first term matches the height at a, the second the slope, the third the bend, the fourth how the bend changes.
  • The polynomial: stop after a few terms and you have a polynomial, which needs only adding and multiplying. Near a, it is very close to the curve.
  • Where it works: more terms hug the curve further out. For some curves the series only works within a fixed distance of a, however many terms you take.

On this page the first curve is sin x around a = 0: x − x³/6 + x⁵/120 − x⁷/5040. With x = 37° = 0.6458 these four terms give 0.6018150, right to seven decimal places.

The terms at x

Try this

  1. Add the terms of sin x one at a time. Each new term bends the polynomial back towards the curve a little further out, and the green band, where it is within 0.01, keeps growing.
  2. Work out sin(37°) like a calculator. x = 0.6458. The terms are 0.64577 − 0.044883 + 0.00093587 − 0.0000092923 = 0.6018150. They shrink so fast that four are enough for seven decimals.
  3. Add up eˣ at x = 1. 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/24 + … The total closes in on e = 2.71828: 10 terms give 2.7182818.
  4. Push ln(1 + x) past x = 1. With all 24 terms the series is excellent up to 1 and useless beyond it: at x = 1.5 it gives −412.9 instead of 0.916. More terms make it worse.
  5. Try arctan x around 0, then drag a to 2. The curve is smooth everywhere, yet around 0 the series fails past 1. The trouble is at the imaginary numbers ±i, at distance √(1 + a²) from a: move a to 2 and it works up to 2.24 away.

From one point to the whole curve, step by step

These pictures use the curve, the point a, the number of terms and the x you chose above.

1Match the height, then the slope

The simplest guess is a flat line at the curve's height at a: the first term, f(a). It is right at a and soon wrong.

Add the second term, f′(a)·(x − a): a slope. Now the line is the curve's tangent at a. Over a step of length x − a it rises f′(a) times that step, which is the slope triangle in the picture.

2Match the bend

A straight line can't bend. The third term, f″(a)/2·(x − a)², is a parabola: it makes the slope change at the same rate as the curve's slope changes at a.

The picture shows the miss at your x after each term: the gap between the curve and the polynomial. Every term you add makes the miss much smaller, as long as x is close enough to a.

3Where the factorials come from

Take the derivative of (x − a)⁴ again and again. Each time the power comes down in front and drops by one: 4·(x − a)³, then 4·3·(x − a)², then 4·3·2·(x − a), then 4·3·2·1 = 24.

So the fourth derivative of the term c₄·(x − a)⁴ is 24·c₄. To make it equal the curve's fourth derivative at a, c₄ must be f⁗(a) ÷ 24. In general cₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(a) ÷ k!, where k! (k factorial) is 1·2·3·…·k.

4Add the terms up, one by one

At your x, each term is just a number. The bars are those numbers; the line is the running total, closing in on the true value of the curve (dashed).

Near a the terms shrink fast, because each has a bigger factorial underneath and a higher power of a small distance on top. That is why a calculator needs only a handful of them.

5Too far out, the terms grow

For ln(1 + x), 1 ÷ (1 − x) and arctan x, the terms at x are roughly (x − a)ᵏ ÷ Rᵏ, for a fixed distance R. Closer than R, they shrink and the sum settles. Further than R, they grow, and adding more only makes it worse.

R is the radius of convergence: the distance from a to the nearest point where the curve breaks down. For ln(1 + x) that is x = −1; for arctan x it is the imaginary numbers ±i, which is why a smooth curve can still have a short radius.

6How big is the error?

Near a, the error is about the size of the first term you left out. For sin x it is never more than that: no derivative of sin is ever bigger than 1, so the error is at most |x − a|ⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1)!.

That is how a calculator knows when to stop: it adds terms until the next one is smaller than the last digit it shows.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: f(x) is the height of the curve at x, and a is the point the series is built around. f′(a), f″(a) and f⁽ᵏ⁾(a) are the first, second and k-th derivatives at a: the slope, how fast the slope changes, and so on. k! (k factorial) is 1·2·3·…·k, with 0! = 1. cₖ (c sub k) is the number in front of the k-th term, and Pₙ(x) is the polynomial with terms up to power n.

Pₙ(x) = f(a) + f′(a)·(x − a) + f″(a)/2!·(x − a)² + … + f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n!·(x − a)ⁿ

In words: start from the height at a, then add the slope, the bend and so on, each times a power of the distance from a.

cₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(a) ÷ k!

In words: the k-th number is the k-th derivative at a, divided by k factorial, because the k-th derivative of (x − a)ᵏ is k!.

sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − x⁷/7! + …, eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …

In words: the series of sin and of eˣ around a = 0; both work for every x.

|sin x − Pₙ(x)| ≤ |x − a|ⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1)!

In words: for sin, the error is never more than the size of the first term left out, if its derivative there were 1.

Where you meet it

  • Your calculator and every computer. sin, cos, eˣ and ln on a chip are worked out with polynomials (or with a related method of adds and shifts called CORDIC). The polynomials are usually tuned versions of the Taylor polynomial, spreading the error evenly over a range.
  • A swinging pendulum. Physics books replace sin θ with θ, the first term, when the swing is small. That turns a hard equation into an easy one and gives the familiar period 2π·√(length ÷ g).
  • E = mc² and the kinetic energy. Einstein's energy of a moving body, written as a series in its speed, starts mc² + ½·m·v² + …: the second term is the kinetic energy from school physics.
  • Training AI models. Gradient descent treats the error as a straight line (the first two terms) around the current settings; Newton's method uses a parabola (the first three).
  • Bonds and loans. Traders estimate how a bond's price changes with interest rates by its "duration" and "convexity": the slope and bend terms of a Taylor series.

Fun facts

Kerala, around 1400

Madhava of Sangamagrama, who founded the Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics in India, found the series for sin, cos and arctan around 1400, about 250 years before they were found in Europe.

Taylor, 1715

Brook Taylor published the general method in Methodus Incrementorum Directa et Inversa in 1715. James Gregory had known it in 1671 but did not publish it.

Maclaurin's name

A Taylor series around a = 0 is often called a Maclaurin series, after Colin Maclaurin, who used them heavily in his Treatise of Fluxions of 1742.

A very slow π

arctan 1 = π/4 gives π = 4·(1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …). It is beautiful and almost useless: after 1,000 terms it gives 3.14059, still wrong in the third decimal place.

A curve its series can't see

In 1823 Augustin-Louis Cauchy noticed that e^(−1/x²) (with value 0 at x = 0) has every derivative 0 at 0. Its Taylor series is 0 + 0 + 0 + …, yet the curve itself is not 0.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "More terms always give a better answer." But: only within the radius of convergence. Beyond it, each extra term makes the answer worse.
  • Not: "A smooth curve's series works everywhere." But: arctan x is smooth everywhere, and its series around 0 still fails past 1, because of trouble at the imaginary numbers ±i.
  • Not: "x³/3! means x³ divided by 3." But: 3! is 3 factorial, 1·2·3 = 6. The factorials grow so fast that later terms get tiny.
  • Not: "The Taylor polynomial is the best polynomial for the job." But: it is the best very near a. Over a whole range, calculators use polynomials tuned so the worst error is as small as possible.
