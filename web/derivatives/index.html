What your speedometer actually measures

Speed is how fast distance changes; distance is all the speed added up.

Here the example is something moving along a line for 10 seconds: where it is, how fast it goes, and how hard it speeds up. Draw on any of the three graphs and the other two follow. Going down a graph is the derivative, going up is the integral.

What derivatives and integrals are

A derivative is a rate of change: how fast one quantity changes as another one moves. On a graph it is the slope at a single point. An integral is the opposite: it adds up a rate over a stretch to get the total, and on a graph it is the area under the curve. The two undo each other, which is the fundamental theorem of calculus.

On this page the quantity is position along a line over time. Its derivative is speed, the derivative of speed is acceleration, and integrating speed gives the distance covered. Everything you can read off a speedometer and an odometer is this pair of operations.

How the graphs are linked, step by step

The pictures below use the motion above. Pick another preset or draw something and they change with it.

1Position: where is it?

The top graph says where the object is at each moment, in metres from the start. Read it like a diary: flat means standing still, going up means moving forward, going down means coming back.

Steep means fast. That one word, steep, is where velocity comes from.

2Velocity is the slope of position

Take two moments, a gap h apart. Distance gained divided by time taken is the average speed over that gap: the slope of the red line.

average speed = (s(t + h) − s(t)) / h

Now shrink the gap. The red line swings round until it just touches the curve: the tangent. Its slope is the velocity at that one instant. That limit is the derivative, written v = ds/dt.

3Acceleration is the slope of velocity

Do the same trick again, this time on the velocity graph. How fast the velocity changes is the acceleration, a = dv/dt, in metres per second, per second.

Positive acceleration means the velocity is going up. Braking while driving forward is negative acceleration. Going round in circles at a steady speed? In two dimensions that's acceleration too, because the direction changes.

And you can keep going: the slope of acceleration is called jerk. It's what you feel when a bus pulls away suddenly.

4Going back up: the area under the curve

If you know the velocity, how far did you go? Over a short moment, distance = velocity × time: a thin rectangle under the velocity graph. Add up all the thin rectangles.

The thinner they get, the closer the sum comes to the exact area: the integral. Area under velocity is distance travelled; area under acceleration is the change in velocity. Area below the axis counts as negative: going backwards.

5Why your drawing needs smoothing

Any hand-drawn line wobbles a little. A wobble is a tiny, steep up-and-down, and the slope only cares about steepness. So velocity turns a small wobble into a big one, and acceleration turns that into chaos.

Area does the opposite: adding things up averages wobbles away. That's why drawing on the acceleration graph gives silky position curves, while drawing position needs the smoothing slider. Try it with smoothing at 0.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: s(t) is the position at time t, v speed, a acceleration. h is a short stretch of time. ds/dt is read "the rate of change of s with t" (not s divided by d·t); d²s/dt² is the rate of change of that rate. ∫₀ᵀ … dt means "add up, from time 0 to time T".

f′(t) = lim (f(t + h) − f(t)) / h as h → 0

In words: the derivative is the rise over the run, for a run so short it's a single instant.

v = ds/dt a = dv/dt = d²s/dt²

In words: speed is the derivative of position, and acceleration is the derivative of speed, so the second derivative of position.

s(T) = s(0) + ∫₀ᵀ v(t) dt

In words: where you end up is where you started plus the area under the speed graph. Area below the axis counts as going backwards.

d/dT ∫₀ᵀ f(t) dt = f(T)

In words: the fundamental theorem of calculus. Take the area, then its slope, and you get the graph you started with. The two operations undo each other.

Where you meet it

  • Your car. The speedometer shows a derivative (distance per second); the trip counter shows an integral (speed added up over time).
  • Your phone. Its accelerometer measures acceleration; integrating it twice is how step counters and games estimate movement.
  • Lifts, trains and roller coasters. Engineers limit jerk, the derivative of acceleration, so the ride feels smooth instead of a jolt.
  • Money. Interest is a rate of change of your balance; the balance is the integral of everything paid in and out.
  • Medicine. How much of a drug your body has taken in is the area under its blood-level curve, a number doctors call the AUC.

Fun facts

Galileo's ramps

Galileo rolled balls down gentle ramps and found the distance grows with the square of the time: 1, 4, 9, 16. He published it in 1638, long before anyone had calculus to explain why.

Two inventors, one big row

Newton and Leibniz each invented calculus in the late 1600s, independently. They argued for years over who was first. We still write dy/dx, which is Leibniz's notation.

Snap, crackle, pop

After velocity, acceleration and jerk, the next slopes are nicknamed snap, crackle and pop. Engineers really do limit jerk in lifts, trains and roller coasters so rides feel smooth.

The fastest human

Usain Bolt reached about 12.4 m/s (44.7 km/h) during his 2009 world-record 100 m. Above, that would be a position line rising 12.4 metres every second.

What a fast car feels like

A car that goes from 0 to 100 km/h in 5 seconds accelerates at about 5.6 m/s² on average, a bit more than half of what gravity does to a falling stone (9.81 m/s²).

Pilots and g

Fighter pilots can take about 9 g, nine times the pull of gravity, for a few seconds, with special suits and breathing. Most people pass out somewhere around 4 to 6 g.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "Zero speed means zero acceleration." But: a ball at the top of its throw has zero speed and is still accelerating downwards at 9.81 m/s².
  • Not: "Negative acceleration means slowing down." But: it means the speed is going down. If you're already going backwards, that makes you go faster.
  • Not: "The graph shows the path." But: a position-time graph shows where along a line, against time. A curve going up and down is moving forwards and backwards, not over a hill.
