Vectors and the dot product

A vector is a list of numbers and an arrow; the dot product measures how much two arrows point the same way.

Every piece of data an AI touches is a vector: a picture, a word, a song, you. Drag the two arrows below and watch the one number that compares them. Then switch to A neuron and see that the most basic building block of a neural network is nothing but a dot product.

From a list of numbers to a neuron, step by step

These pictures follow what is in the toy above: change it and they change.

1A vector is a list of numbers, and an arrow

The vector a = (3, 1) is just two numbers. Drawn as an arrow, it means: go 3 across, then 1 up. A list of 3 numbers is an arrow in 3D space, and a list of 1,000 numbers is an arrow in a space with 1,000 directions. We can't picture that, but the maths works exactly the same.

A grid of numbers is a matrix, and a stack of grids (like the red, green and blue layers of a photo) is a tensor. AI software is named after them: TensorFlow, PyTorch tensors.

2How long is an arrow? It depends on how you measure

The usual length is the straight line, from Pythagoras: √(3² + 1²) ≈ 3.16. That is the L² norm. If you can only walk along streets, the length is 3 + 1 = 4: the L¹ norm. If only the biggest step counts, it is 3: the L∞ norm.

Machine learning uses all three. L² is the ordinary distance; L¹ is used to make models prefer using only a few inputs; the "size" of a whole matrix (the Frobenius norm) is just L² over all its numbers.

3The dot product: multiply matching parts, then add

To compare two arrows, multiply their first numbers together, their second numbers together, and add it all up. That single number is the dot product, a·b. It works the same for lists of any length.

Big and positive means they point roughly the same way. Zero means they are at right angles. Negative means they point away from each other.

4It is really about the angle between them

The dot product also equals |a| × |b| × cos θ, where θ is the angle between the arrows. Turn b around a full circle and the dot product rises and falls like a wave: largest when they line up, zero at right angles, most negative when opposite.

So the dot product mixes two things: how long the arrows are and how well they line up. The shadow of b on a (the dashed line in the toy) has length a·b / |a|.

5Cosine similarity: how AI compares meaning

Divide the dot product by both lengths and only the angle is left: cos θ, between −1 and 1. This is cosine similarity. Language models turn every word, sentence or picture into a long vector, an embedding, so that things with similar meaning point in similar directions.

Search, recommendations and "find similar" all work this way: turn the question into a vector and look for the stored vectors with the highest cosine similarity.

6A neuron is a dot product with a threshold

A single artificial neuron takes the inputs x, takes the dot product with its weights w, adds a bias b, and says yes if the result is above zero. All points where w·x + b = 0 form a straight line (in more dimensions, a flat plane): one side is yes, the other no.

Learning means nudging w and b whenever the neuron gets a point wrong. Rosenblatt's perceptron did exactly this in 1958. A modern network stacks millions of these, with a bend between layers.

In symbols

a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + aₙbₙ

In words: multiply the matching numbers and add them up.

a·b = |a| |b| cos θ

In words: the same number, from the lengths and the angle between the arrows.

‖a‖₂ = √(a·a), ‖a‖₁ = Σ|aᵢ|, ‖a‖∞ = max|aᵢ|

In words: three ways to measure length: straight line, city blocks, biggest part.

cos θ = a·b / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

In words: cosine similarity: only the direction counts, not the length.

proj = (a·b / a·a) a

In words: the shadow of b on the line of a.

y = step(w·x + b)

In words: a neuron: weigh the inputs, add the bias, say yes above zero.

Where you meet it

  • Search and recommendations. Shops, music apps and search engines turn items and questions into embedding vectors and rank by cosine similarity.
  • Chatbots. Inside a transformer, every word asks a question (a query vector) and every other word holds a key vector; their dot products decide where it pays attention.
  • Every neuron. Each neuron in a neural network computes a dot product of its inputs and its weights.
  • 3D games. How brightly a surface is lit is the dot product of its facing direction and the direction of the light.
  • Physics. Work done by a force is the dot product of the force and the movement: pushing sideways on a moving cart does no work.

Fun facts

The perceptron, 1958

Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron learned to sort inputs into two groups by adjusting the weights in a dot product. It was one of the first machines that learned from examples.

King − man + woman ≈ queen

In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google showed that word vectors learned from text (word2vec) capture meaning: subtracting and adding them gives answers like this.

Attention is dot products

The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the transformer, the design behind today's chatbots. Its attention scores are dot products, divided by the square root of the vector length to keep them from growing too large.

Light and angles, 1760

Johann Heinrich Lambert described in 1760 that a matte surface looks dimmer the more it is tilted from the light, by the cosine of the angle. Games still light surfaces with exactly this dot product.

Vectors are younger than you think

The dot and cross product notation we use today comes from Josiah Willard Gibbs's lecture notes on vector analysis, printed for his students at Yale in the early 1880s.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: "The dot product of two vectors is a vector." But: it is a single number. (The cross product, in 3D, is the one that gives a vector.)
  • Not: "A dot product of zero means one of them is zero." But: it usually means they are at right angles.
  • Not: "A big dot product means very similar." But: longer arrows give bigger dot products too. That is why AI usually compares with cosine similarity, which ignores length.
