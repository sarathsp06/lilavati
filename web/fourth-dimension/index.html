The fourth dimension

What a four-dimensional cube looks like from here

A cube’s shadow is flat. A tesseract’s shadow is a cube. Turn it and watch the shadow change.

Below is the shadow of a tesseract, the four-dimensional cube, cast into our space. Turn it in the three planes that use the fourth direction and the small inner cube swells into the outer one while the outer one shrinks inside, yet nothing in the real shape grows or bends. Then build it yourself, from a point, by dragging copies.

What the fourth dimension is

The fourth dimension, in geometry, is a fourth independent direction: one at right angles to left–right, front–back and up–down all at once. A point in four-dimensional space needs four numbers, (x, y, z, w), where w is the new one, just as a point on a page needs two and a point in a room needs three. Lengths, angles and turns work exactly as in 3D, with one more term in every formula.

We can’t point in that direction, so we study four-dimensional shapes by their numbers and by their projections: shadows cast into our space, the way a cube casts a flat shadow on a wall. On this page the shape is the tesseract (four-dimensional cube), and the picture is its shadow.

Try this

  1. Slide xw from 0° to 180°. The inner cube grows, passes through, and ends up outside, while the outer one shrinks inside. A half turn in the xw plane swaps w for −w: near and far trade places.
  2. Turn only in our space (xz). The shadow turns like any 3D object; nothing turns inside out. Turns in xy, xz or yz never touch w, so the inner cube stays inner.
  3. Move the eye far away (d = 8), then close (d = 1.8). Far away, the two cubes are almost the same size. Close up, the far cube is tiny. The shadow divides by the distance to the eye.
  4. Colour by the two cubes and keep turning. The orange cube (w = −½) and the teal one (w = +½) take turns being inside. The readout says every one of the 32 edges is still exactly 1 long: only the shadow stretches.

How to build and see a tesseract, step by step

These pictures follow what you set up above: change the turns or the eye distance and they change.

1Each new dimension is a copy dragged in a new direction

Drag a point and its path is a segment. Drag the segment sideways, at right angles, and it sweeps out a square. Drag the square straight up and you get a cube. Every corner traces a new edge as it goes.

Drag the cube in a fourth direction, at right angles to all three, and you get the tesseract. We can’t do that last drag in our room, but the numbers can: every corner just gets a fourth coordinate w that goes from −½ to +½. Drag across the picture to pull the copy.

2Counting: corners double, edges double plus one per corner

The new shape is the old one, its copy, and the paths between them. So it has twice the corners. Its edges are the old edges, the copy’s edges, and one new edge for every corner that moved.

A square has 4 corners and 4 edges, so a cube has 2·4 = 8 corners and 2·4 + 4 = 12 edges. A cube has 8 corners and 12 edges, so a tesseract has 2·8 = 16 corners and 2·12 + 8 = 32 edges. Watch them counted on the shape you picked in step 1.

3A shadow divides by the distance to the eye

Seen from the side, a light ray goes from each corner to the eye. Where it crosses the screen, one step in front of the eye, is the corner’s shadow. By similar triangles, a corner at height x and a distance d − z from the eye lands at height x · 1/(d − z): far things look small.

One dimension up it is the same rule with w as the depth: the eye sits at w = d, and a corner lands at x · 1/(d − w). The cube at w = −½ is farther away, so its shadow is the small inner cube; the one at w = +½ is the big outer one. All eight cubes of the tesseract are the same size; only their shadows differ.

4A turn in the xw plane trades x for w

A turn always happens in a flat plane: in 3D a turn about an “axis” is really a turn in the plane at right angles to it. In 4D there are six such planes: xy, xz, yz (our ordinary turns) and xw, yw, zw. A turn by an angle θ (theta) in the xw plane changes only x and w, exactly like turning a picture on a page, and leaves y and z alone.

So turning moves corners nearer and farther in w, and the shadow rule from step 3 makes them grow and shrink. The corner keeps its distance from the middle: the shape is rigid, only its shadow changes.

5A slice is what someone living in 3D would see

A creature living on a flat sheet, watching a cube pass through its world, sees only where the cube meets the sheet: a square, or a triangle or hexagon if the cube is tilted, that appears, changes shape and vanishes. Each edge that crosses the sheet gives one corner of the slice.

We would see a tesseract passing through our space the same way: a solid that appears, changes shape and vanishes. Here the tesseract, turned as you set it above, is cut by our space at the height w = h.

In symbols

Reading the symbols: x, y, z, w are a point’s four coordinates, how far it is along each of four directions at right angles. n is the number of dimensions; V(n) and E(n) are how many corners (vertices) and edges the n-dimensional cube has. θ (theta) is an angle of turn. d is how far the eye is from the middle of the shape, measured along w. A dot · means times; x² means x · x, and 2ⁿ means n twos multiplied together.

V(n) = 2 · V(n − 1) = 2ⁿ
E(n) = 2 · E(n − 1) + V(n − 1) = n · 2ⁿ⁻¹

In words: dragging a copy doubles the corners, and the edges are twice the old edges plus one path per old corner. For the tesseract, n = 4: 16 corners and 32 edges.

length of (x, y, z, w) = √(x² + y² + z² + w²)

In words: Pythagoras, with one more square under the root. Each corner of the tesseract (±½, ±½, ±½, ±½) is exactly 1 from the middle.

x′ = cos(θ) · x − sin(θ) · w
w′ = sin(θ) · x + cos(θ) · w
y′ = y, z′ = z

In words: a turn by θ in the xw plane mixes x and w like turning a page, and leaves y and z as they were. The primes (x′, “x new”) are the coordinates after the turn.

(x, y, z, w) → (x, y, z) · 1/(d − w)

In words: the shadow in our space keeps x, y and z, divided by the distance d − w from the eye, so corners far away in w come out small.

Where you meet it

  • 3D graphics in every game. Graphics cards store each point of a 3D scene as four numbers (x, y, z, w), called homogeneous coordinates, and make perspective by dividing by w: the same division as step 3.
  • How your phone knows which way it is turned. Phones, game cameras and spacecraft store orientations as quaternions: four numbers whose squares add to 1, a point on a sphere in four-dimensional space.
  • Data and machine learning. Anything described by four measurements is a point in 4D: the classic iris flower data set measures each flower’s petal and sepal length and width. Programs compare such points with the same √ distance formula, in four or four thousand dimensions.
  • Physics. Relativity describes events by four numbers, three of place and one of time. That spacetime is four-dimensional, but its distance has a minus sign in it, so it is a different geometry from the one on this page.

Fun facts

The word, 1888

Charles Howard Hinton coined “tesseract” in his 1888 book A New Era of Thought, and built sets of coloured cubes to train people to picture four-dimensional space.

Flatland, 1884

Edwin Abbott Abbott’s novella Flatland tells of a Square living in a flat world who is visited by a Sphere: he sees only a circle that grows and shrinks, as in step 5.

A painted tesseract, 1954

Salvador Dalí’s Crucifixion (Corpus Hypercubus) shows a tesseract unfolded into eight cubes, as a cube unfolds into six squares. It hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Six perfect shapes

3D has five regular solids. Around 1852 Ludwig Schläfli showed that 4D has six, including the 24-cell, which has no 3D cousin, and every dimension from five upward has only three.

No knots in 4D

Any knotted loop of string can be untied in four dimensions without cutting it: one strand steps aside in w while another passes where it was.

Carved into a bridge, 1843

On 16 October 1843 William Rowan Hamilton found the rule for quaternions, four-number rotations, while walking in Dublin and carved it into Broom Bridge. A plaque marks the spot today.

Common mix-ups

  • Not: “a tesseract is a small cube inside a big one.” But: that is its shadow. All eight of its cubes are the same size; the inner one is just farther away in w.
  • Not: “the shape bends and grows as it turns.” But: every edge stays exactly 1 long. The shadow stretches, the same way a turning cube’s shadow does on a wall.
  • Not: “the fourth dimension is time.” But: time is a fourth coordinate in physics, but in geometry the fourth dimension is simply a fourth direction of space, with the same distance formula as the other three.
